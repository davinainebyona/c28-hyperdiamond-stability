Take diamond's lattice and replace every atom with a 28-atom carbon cage. It exists in density-functional calculations, not yet in a lab. The model is built from the published bond lengths.

What would be unusual about it.

Calculated

A third of diamond's density, and still one bonded network

Solid C60 is also light, but its cages are only held together by weak van der Waals attraction. In hyperdiamond every cage is chemically bonded to four neighbours, so the whole crystal is a single covalent framework.

Calculated

A semiconductor where diamond is an insulator

The calculated gap is 1.61 eV, and the real value is expected nearer 3 eV. Diamond's is 5.47 eV. A 3 eV gap corresponds to light of about 413 nm, at the violet edge of the visible range.

Measured for single cages

Every cage is a container

C28 has been detected with a titanium, zirconium or uranium atom trapped inside. In a crystal that would give one dopant position per 28 carbons, placed by the lattice itself. Nobody has tested that in a solid.

Related calculation

A superconductor candidate

C28 is more sharply curved than C60, which couples its electrons to vibrations more than twice as strongly in calculations. For a hydrogen-capped relative with sodium inside each cage, Romero and co-workers estimated a transition near 58 K, against 19 K measured for K3C60. That is a different solid, and nothing has been measured.

Calculated geometry

Mostly empty space

Switch to the crystal view and look along a channel. The open framework led Kaxiras and co-workers to suggest a disordered version as a storage medium.

What it would not be

Harder than diamond

Of the 352 bonds in a cubic cell only 16 join one cage to another, and the calculated stiffness is far below diamond's. It is also 0.744 eV per atom less stable than diamond, so it could only exist as a metastable material. That alone does not rule it out: C60 sits roughly 0.4 eV per atom above graphite and is made in bulk.

How you could do it.

Nobody can make crystalline hyperdiamond today, in a school or a national lab. Every result about it comes from computing it, and that part is open to you with free software.

  1. Build and inspect it An afternoon

    Copy the structure file below into a text file named hyperdiamond.cif and open it in VESTA or Avogadro 2. Measure the four bond lengths and the 109.47° angle between cage centres, and check them against the table.

  2. Relax a single cage A weekend

    Run the free quantum-chemistry program xtb on bare C28 and on C28H4, where a hydrogen caps each apex. The literature says capping the four open bonds turns an unstable open-shell cage into a stable molecule. See whether your calculation agrees.

  3. Compute the crystal A few weeks, a good EPQ

    Use Quantum ESPRESSO (free; on Windows it runs under WSL) on the 56-atom primitive cell. Calculate the energy at several cell sizes, fit the curve to get the lattice constant and bulk modulus, then plot the band structure. Your targets are 15.777 Å and a gap near 1.6 eV. Reproducing a published result independently is real research practice.

  4. What a laboratory would need University facility

    A pulsed laser vaporising graphite into helium makes carbon clusters. A mass filter set to 336 u (28 × 12) picks out C28, and the beam is landed gently on a cold surface in ultra-high vacuum. Simulations by Canning, Galli and Kim in 1997 found the cages survive landing and link up, but into a disordered network. This needs a class 4 laser and vacuum equipment, so it is not a home or school experiment.

  5. The unsolved part Open problem

    A crystal needs every cage in exactly the right orientation, and Kaxiras and co-workers argued that an amorphous solid is the more likely outcome. There is a second catch. The metal atom that makes C28 survivable donates four electrons to the cage, which uses up the very open bonds the crystal relies on.

Geometry check. The cage was solved from the four relaxed bond lengths and the 2.57 Å non-bonded contact in Zeger et al., with exact Td symmetry. It has 12 pentagons, 4 hexagons and 42 bonds.
